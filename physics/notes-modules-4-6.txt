MODULE 4: SIGNAL INTEGRITY & EMI
================================================================

1. DEFINITIONS
   1.1 Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)
       - Unwanted electrical noise coupling from one circuit to another
       - Sources: switching power supplies, motors, digital clocks, lightning
       - Victims: sensitive analog signals, communication links, navigation sensors
       - Q: Why is EMI worse at higher frequencies?

   1.2 Crosstalk
       - Signal coupling between adjacent wires via capacitance (electric field) or mutual inductance (magnetic field)
       - Capacitive: dominant at high impedance, high frequency
       - Inductive: dominant at low impedance, high current
       - Q: Two parallel wires 1cm apart for 5m. How much coupling?

   1.3 Shielding Effectiveness (SE)
       - SE = 20*log10(E_unshielded / E_shielded) in dB
       - Good shield: SE > 60dB (1000:1 reduction)
       - Depends on: material, thickness, frequency, apertures (holes)
       - Q: 40dB shielding means noise reduced by what factor? (100x)

   1.4 Common Mode vs Differential Mode
       - Differential: signal travels on one wire, returns on another (intended)
       - Common mode: noise appears equally on both wires relative to ground (unintended)
       - Twisted pairs reject common-mode noise (each twist cancels the previous)

2. EMI CONTROL TECHNIQUES
   2.1 Separation: keep noisy wires away from sensitive wires
       - AS50881 requires minimum separation distances by category
       - Category 1 (power): separate from Category 3 (signal) by >2 inches
   2.2 Shielding: surround sensitive wires with conductive layer
       - Braid: 85-95% coverage, flexible
       - Foil: 100% coverage but fragile
       - Must be grounded at one or both ends (depends on frequency)
   2.3 Twisted pair: reject magnetic field coupling
       - More twists per foot = better rejection
       - Standard: 2-4 twists per inch for aerospace
   2.4 Filtering: block noise at the source or receiver
       - Ferrite beads, capacitors to ground, LC filters
   2.5 Grounding: provide low-impedance return path
       - Single-point ground (low freq) vs multi-point ground (high freq)

3. AEROSPACE STANDARDS
   3.1 MIL-STD-461G: EMI requirements for military electronics
       - CE102: conducted emissions on power leads (150kHz-10MHz)
       - RE102: radiated emissions (10kHz-18GHz)
       - CS114: conducted susceptibility (bulk cable injection)
       - RS103: radiated susceptibility (electric field)
   3.2 DO-160G: environmental conditions for airborne equipment
       - Section 20: RF susceptibility
       - Section 21: emission of RF energy
   3.3 Q: What happens if a wire harness fails EMI testing? (Redesign: add shielding, increase separation, add filtering. Costs months.)

4. CALCULATION: CROSSTALK BETWEEN PARALLEL WIRES
   - Mutual capacitance: C_m ~ pi*epsilon/ln(d/r) per meter
   - d = separation, r = wire radius
   - Coupled voltage: V_crosstalk = Z_victim * C_m * L * dV_source/dt
   - Q: For a 1MHz clock signal (5V, 1ns rise), 10cm parallel run, 1cm separation: estimate crosstalk

5. HISTORICAL
   - James Clerk Maxwell (1831-1879): unified electricity and magnetism (Maxwell's equations, 1865)
       - Predicted electromagnetic waves travel at speed of light
       - Foundation of ALL EMI theory
   - Heinrich Hertz (1857-1894): first to produce and detect radio waves (1887)
       - Proved Maxwell's prediction experimentally
   - Nikola Tesla (1856-1943): AC power, radio, wireless transmission
       - His work on resonance is directly relevant to EMI (resonant structures amplify noise)
   - EMI in aerospace: Apollo 12 struck by lightning at launch (1969)
       - Momentarily lost all telemetry. Recovered by resetting flight computer.
       - Led to comprehensive lightning protection standards for all future spacecraft

MODULE 5: POWER DISTRIBUTION & REDUNDANCY
================================================================

1. DEFINITIONS
   1.1 Power Bus
       - Conductor (wire or bus bar) that distributes power from source to loads
       - Primary bus: main power (solar panels or generators)
       - Secondary bus: regulated, distributed to subsystems
       - Essential bus: stays powered even during faults (critical loads only)

   1.2 Redundancy
       - Single-fault tolerant: system survives any one failure
       - Dual-fault tolerant: survives any two failures (human-rated spacecraft)
       - Q: ISS has quad-redundant power channels. Why?

   1.3 Single Point Failure (SPF)
       - Any single component whose failure causes mission loss
       - SPF analysis: trace every power path. If ONE wire/fuse/relay fails and kills the mission, it's an SPF.
       - Q: A sensor has one power wire from one fuse on one bus. Is this an SPF? (Yes)

   1.4 Circuit Protection
       - Fuses: one-time overcurrent protection (blow and require replacement)
       - Circuit breakers: resettable overcurrent protection
       - Current limiters: electronic, active protection
       - Q: Why do spacecraft prefer electronic protection over fuses? (Can't replace a fuse in orbit)

2. BUS ARCHITECTURES
   2.1 Centralized: one power distribution unit (PDU), all loads branch from it
       - Simple, but PDU is an SPF
   2.2 Distributed: multiple PDUs, loads spread across them
       - More wiring but more survivable
   2.3 Ring bus: power can flow in either direction around a loop
       - Any single cut still delivers power to all loads
   2.4 Cross-strapping: connect redundant buses so either can power critical loads
       - Standard in spacecraft: Bus A and Bus B, critical loads on both

3. POWER BUDGET
   - Total generation must exceed total consumption (with margin)
   - Margin: typically 10-20% for spacecraft, 30% during eclipse
   - Load profile: varies with mission phase (launch, cruise, science, comm)
   - Q: 6 subsystems averaging 5W each, 28V bus. Total current? (30W / 28V = 1.07A)
   - Q: If solar panels produce 60W and loads consume 45W, what margin? (33%)

4. FAILURE MODE ANALYSIS
   - Open circuit: wire breaks, current stops (load loses power)
   - Short circuit: insulation failure, current spikes (protection device activates)
   - Intermittent: connection loosens, power flickers (hardest to diagnose)
   - Cascading failure: one failure overloads adjacent circuits, causing more failures
   - Q: A short on Bus A trips its fuse. All Bus A loads die. If comm is only on Bus A, what happens? (Loss of communication -- design error)

5. HISTORICAL
   - Apollo 13 (1970): oxygen tank explosion severed primary power bus
       - Crew survived because Lunar Module had INDEPENDENT power system
       - Redundancy saved three lives
   - Hubble Space Telescope: two solar array wings, two battery sets, cross-strapped buses
       - Any single power failure: full science continues
   - Mars Polar Lander (1999): lost due to premature engine shutdown
       - Spurious signal on a single wire interpreted as touchdown
       - Single-point failure in sensing, not power, but same analysis discipline

6. REFERENCES
   - Space Vehicle Design (Griffin & French), Ch 10 (Power Systems)
   - Spacecraft Systems Engineering (Fortescue), Ch 10
   - NASA Systems Engineering Handbook SP-2016-6105, Section 6.3
   - MIL-STD-1576 (Electroexplosive Subsystem Safety)

MODULE 6: CONNECTORS & PIN-LEVEL DESIGN
================================================================

1. DEFINITIONS
   1.1 Connector
       - Mechanical device that joins two wire bundles while maintaining electrical contact
       - Mating pair: plug (male pins) and receptacle (female sockets)
       - Contact: individual metal element (pin or socket) that carries one signal

   1.2 Pin Assignment
       - Mapping of signals/power to specific pin positions in a connector
       - Must consider: current capacity per pin, separation of power from signal, spare allocation
       - Q: Why leave spare pins? (Future modifications without new connectors)

   1.3 Contact Resistance
       - Resistance of the metal-to-metal junction at a pin
       - Typically 1-10 milliohms for aerospace connectors
       - Increases with age, vibration, contamination
       - At high current: P = I^2 * R_contact -> heating

   1.4 Keying / Polarization
       - Mechanical features that prevent mating the wrong connector
       - Prevents catastrophic cross-wiring (e.g., power into signal pin)

2. AEROSPACE CONNECTOR TYPES
   2.1 MIL-DTL-38999 (most common aerospace circular connector)
       - Bayonet or threaded coupling
       - Environmental sealing (waterproof, pressurized)
       - Contact sizes: 22 (signal, 5A), 20 (7.5A), 16 (13A), 12 (23A)
       - Shell sizes: 9 (5 contacts) to 25 (128 contacts)
       - Q: A connector with sixty 22-size contacts. Max total current if all loaded? (60 * 5A = 300A theoretical, but derated for bundling)

   2.2 D-Sub (older, commercial)
       - Trapezoidal shell, 9 to 50 pins
       - Lower vibration resistance than MIL-38999
       - Used in avionics for legacy interfaces

   2.3 Molex / Nano connectors
       - Very small, lightweight
       - Used inside spacecraft where vibration is low but space is tight

   2.4 RF Connectors (SMA, TNC, N-type)
       - For coaxial (shielded) cables carrying radio frequency signals
       - Impedance-matched (50 or 75 ohms)
       - Critical for communication and radar systems

3. PIN ASSIGNMENT RULES
   3.1 Power pins: locate near shell (shortest path to ground shield)
   3.2 Sensitive signals: center of connector (maximum separation from shell/power)
   3.3 Return/ground: adjacent to every signal (minimize loop area for EMI)
   3.4 Spares: minimum 10-20% spare contacts for future growth
   3.5 No adjacent high-current and low-signal pins (crosstalk)
   3.6 Document EVERYTHING: pin-out drawing is a controlled document

4. CONSTRAINT CHECKING (WHAT ARTIFACT DOES)
   - For each connector:
     * Total current through all pins <= connector rating (derated for temperature)
     * No single pin exceeds its contact rating
     * Power and signal separation meets EMI requirements
     * All grounds assigned and connected
     * Spare count meets minimum
     * Mating connector pin-out matches (plug to receptacle)
   - For the system:
     * Every signal has a source and destination (no orphan pins)
     * Redundant signals on separate connectors (no SPF at connector level)
     * Wire gauge compatible with contact size (can't crimp 12AWG into size-22 contact)

5. HISTORICAL
   - Cannon Electric (now ITT): invented the circular connector for WWII aircraft
       - The "Cannon plug" became MIL-C-5015, ancestor of MIL-DTL-38999
   - Apollo connectors: zero-g rated, vibration tested to 20g, thermal cycled -150C to +120C
       - Each connector hand-inspected under microscope before flight
   - Challenger (1986): not a connector failure, but O-ring (similar interface problem)
       - Interface design -- where two systems meet -- is where most failures occur
