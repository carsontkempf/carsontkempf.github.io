MODULE 1: DC CIRCUIT FUNDAMENTALS
================================================================

1. DEFINITIONS
   1.1 Voltage (V)
       - Energy per unit charge (Joules/Coulomb)
       - Unit: Volt (V)
       - Q: What physical quantity does voltage represent?
       - Q: Why is voltage a "potential difference" not an absolute?

   1.2 Current (I)
       - Rate of charge flow (Coulombs/second)
       - Unit: Ampere (A)
       - Conventional: positive to negative (historical, opposite to electron flow)
       - Q: If 5 coulombs pass a point in 2 seconds, what is the current?

   1.3 Resistance (R)
       - Opposition to current flow
       - R = rho * L / A (resistivity * length / cross-section area)
       - Unit: Ohm
       - Q: What three physical properties determine a wire's resistance?
       - Q: If you double wire length, what happens to resistance?

   1.4 Power (P)
       - Rate of energy conversion
       - P = V*I = I^2*R = V^2/R
       - Unit: Watt (W)
       - Q: A 28V bus delivers 2A. What power is consumed? (56W)

2. LAWS
   2.1 Ohm's Law: V = I * R
       - Linear relationship (holds for resistive elements only)
       - Q: 10V across 500 ohms. Current? (20mA)
       - Q: When does Ohm's law NOT apply? (diodes, transistors, nonlinear elements)

   2.2 Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)
       - Sum of currents entering a node = sum of currents leaving
       - Conservation of charge
       - Q: Node has 3A in, 1A out on wire B. Current on wire C? (2A out)

   2.3 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)
       - Sum of voltages around any closed loop = 0
       - Conservation of energy
       - Q: 28V source, 0.5V drop in wire 1, 0.3V drop in wire 2. Voltage at load? (27.2V)

3. CIRCUIT TOPOLOGIES
   3.1 Series: R_total = R1 + R2 + ... + Rn
   3.2 Parallel: 1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... + 1/Rn
   3.3 Voltage Divider: V_out = V_in * R2/(R1+R2)
   3.4 Current Divider: I1 = I_total * R2/(R1+R2)

4. AEROSPACE APPLICATION
   - Standard bus: 28V DC (military/space), 115V AC 400Hz (commercial aircraft)
   - AS50881: max 3% voltage drop allowed (28V * 0.03 = 0.84V max)
   - Every connector pin is a node (KCL). Every wire is a branch (KVL).

5. HISTORICAL
   - Georg Ohm (1789-1854): V=IR, rejected for decades, vindicated posthumously
   - Gustav Kirchhoff (1824-1887): KCL/KVL at age 21 (1845), also spectroscopy
   - Alessandro Volta (1745-1827): first battery (voltaic pile, 1800)
   - Andre-Marie Ampere (1775-1836): electrodynamics, force between current-carrying wires
   - WHY 28V: WWII compromise -- low enough to avoid arcing at altitude, high enough to reduce wire weight

6. REFERENCES
   - Alexander & Sadiku, Fundamentals of Electric Circuits, Ch 1-3
   - AllAboutCircuits.com/textbook/direct-current/
   - Hyperphysics: hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/electric/
   - SAE AS50881 (Wiring Aerospace Vehicles)

MODULE 2: WIRE PROPERTIES & VOLTAGE DROP
================================================================

1. DEFINITIONS
   1.1 American Wire Gauge (AWG)
       - Logarithmic scale: smaller number = thicker wire
       - Every 3 gauges doubles cross-section area
       - Every 6 gauges doubles resistance per length
       - Q: Which is thicker, 20AWG or 24AWG? (20AWG)
       - Common aerospace: 20-26 AWG for signals, 12-16 AWG for power

   1.2 Resistivity (rho)
       - Material property: copper = 1.68e-8 ohm-meters at 20C
       - Aluminum = 2.65e-8 (lighter but higher resistance)
       - Q: Why does aerospace prefer copper despite weight penalty?

   1.3 Voltage Drop
       - V_drop = I * R_wire = I * (rho * L / A)
       - L = round-trip length (to load AND back)
       - Q: 5A through 10m of 20AWG copper (0.033 ohm/m). Drop? (5*10*2*0.033 = 3.3V)

   1.4 Temperature Derating
       - Wire resistance increases with temperature: R(T) = R(20C) * [1 + alpha*(T-20)]
       - Copper alpha = 0.00393 per degree C
       - Q: At 85C, how much does copper resistance increase? (~26%)

   1.5 Current Capacity (Ampacity)
       - Maximum current before wire overheats
       - Depends on: gauge, insulation rating, ambient temp, bundling
       - Bundled wires derate (less cooling) -- critical for harnesses

2. MIL-SPEC WIRE TYPES
   2.1 M22759 family (general aerospace)
       - /32: polyimide insulation (lightweight, 200C)
       - /34: crosslinked ETFE (lightweight, 150C)
       - /44: PTFE + polyimide (high-temp, 260C)
   2.2 M27500 (shielded cable)
       - Used for sensitive signals, EMI protection
       - Shield adds weight but required per MIL-STD-461
   2.3 Q: Why are there dozens of wire specs? (Different missions need different trade-offs: weight vs temp vs flexibility vs EMI)

3. DESIGN RULES (AS50881)
   - Max voltage drop: 3% for continuous, 5% for intermittent
   - Wire must handle 1.5x rated current without exceeding temp limit
   - Minimum bend radius: 10x wire diameter
   - No single wire run > 50 feet without intermediate support

4. CALCULATION PRACTICE
   - Given: 28V bus, 8A load, 15m one-way run, 85C ambient
   - Find: minimum AWG to keep drop < 3% (0.84V)
   - R_max = V_drop_max / I = 0.84 / 8 = 0.105 ohms
   - Round trip = 30m, so R_per_m < 0.105/30 = 0.0035 ohm/m
   - At 85C: need R_per_m_at_20C < 0.0035/1.26 = 0.00278 ohm/m
   - 16AWG = 0.013 ohm/m (way oversized), 20AWG = 0.033 (too high!)
   - Answer: need 18AWG (0.021 ohm/m) -- check: 0.021*1.26*30 = 0.79V < 0.84V. PASS.

5. HISTORICAL
   - Joseph Henry (1797-1878): discovered inductance, first electromagnetic motor
   - Wire gauge standardized 1857 (Brown & Sharpe gauge, became AWG)
   - Apollo 11 wiring: 30+ miles of wire, total weight 15kg, all hand-assembled
   - Aerospace wire failures: TWA Flight 800 (1996) -- fuel tank wiring short caused explosion, led to complete rewiring standards overhaul

================================================================
MODULE 3: RC/RL DYNAMICS & TIME CONSTANTS
================================================================

1. DEFINITIONS
   1.1 Capacitor
       - Stores energy in electric field: E = 0.5*C*V^2
       - Q = C*V (charge = capacitance * voltage)
       - Unit: Farad (F); typical: pF to uF
       - Blocks DC, passes AC

   1.2 Inductor
       - Stores energy in magnetic field: E = 0.5*L*I^2
       - V = L * dI/dt (voltage proportional to rate of current change)
       - Unit: Henry (H); typical: uH to mH
       - Passes DC, blocks AC (high frequency)

   1.3 Time Constant (tau)
       - RC circuit: tau = R*C (seconds)
       - RL circuit: tau = L/R (seconds)
       - After 1 tau: 63.2% of final value
       - After 5 tau: 99.3% (considered "settled")
       - Q: R=1k, C=10uF. tau? (10ms). Time to settle? (~50ms)

   1.4 Transfer Function
       - RC low-pass: H(s) = 1/(1 + s*R*C)
       - Cutoff frequency: f_c = 1/(2*pi*R*C)
       - This is the first step toward control systems (Module 7)

2. DYNAMICS (DIFFERENTIAL EQUATIONS)
   2.1 RC Charging: V(t) = V_final * (1 - e^{-t/tau})
   2.2 RC Discharging: V(t) = V_initial * e^{-t/tau}
   2.3 RL Energizing: I(t) = I_final * (1 - e^{-t/tau})
   2.4 RL De-energizing: I(t) = I_initial * e^{-t/tau}
   2.5 Q: Sketch V(t) for charging from 0 to 28V with tau=5ms. What is V at t=5ms? (17.7V)

3. WHY THIS MATTERS FOR AEROSPACE
   - Every wire has parasitic capacitance and inductance
   - Long wire runs create RC time constants that delay signals
   - Inductive loads (motors, solenoids) create voltage spikes when switched off (back-EMF)
   - Protection circuits (snubbers, flyback diodes) tame these transients
   - Q: A 50m shielded cable has 100pF/m capacitance. Total C? With 50 ohm driver, tau? (5nF, 250ns)

4. FILTERING
   4.1 Low-pass (RC): passes slow signals, blocks noise
   4.2 High-pass (CR): passes fast signals, blocks DC offset
   4.3 Band-pass (RLC): passes only a specific frequency range
   4.4 In aerospace: power bus filtering removes switching noise; signal conditioning removes EMI

5. BRIDGE TO CONTROL SYSTEMS
   - A first-order system (one time constant) IS an RC or RL circuit
   - G(s) = 1/(tau*s + 1) -- this is the transfer function of Corbin's fuel metering valve (tau_FMV = 0.03s)
   - Step response of G(s): output = 1 - e^{-t/tau} -- exactly RC charging!
   - The entire gas turbine engine model (Chapter 4) is cascaded first-order systems

6. HISTORICAL
   - Michael Faraday (1791-1867): discovered capacitance, electromagnetic induction
   - Oliver Heaviside (1850-1925): invented circuit analysis math (Laplace transforms, impedance)
       - Self-taught, no degree, rejected by establishment (like Ohm)
       - Developed the telegrapher's equations for signal propagation in cables
   - First transatlantic telegraph cable (1858): failed due to RC time constant!
       - Cable too long, capacitance too high, signals smeared beyond recognition
       - William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) solved it with proper cable theory
